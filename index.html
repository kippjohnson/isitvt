Clinical safety

An undifferentiated wide-complex tachycardia should be presumed VT until proven otherwise. Do not delay resuscitation or cardioversion to run an EKG algorithm.

Basics First

01

Patient first

Unstable?

Assess whether the tachyarrhythmia is causing hypotension, acute altered mental status, signs of shock, ischemic chest discomfort or acute heart failure. Emergent action is required when the rhythm is causing instability.

Unstable monomorphic tachyarrhythmia with a pulse → synchronized cardioversion. Pulseless or polymorphic rhythms require a different resuscitation pathway.
02

Define the rhythm

Regular + monomorphic?

The algorithms collected here were developed primarily for regular monomorphic WCT. Irregular or polymorphic rhythms require a different pathway. Remember that other VTs such as fascicular VTs may not have a wide complex QRS at all.

03

Collect the evidence

Full 12-lead?

Apply these algorithms to a 12-lead EKG recorded during the tachycardia. Compare with baseline when available.

High-value findings

Not all findings mean the same thing. First look for direct evidence that the atria and ventricles are behaving independently, then look for strong clues from activation direction.

Direct evidence of a ventricular rhythm

When confidently identified, these findings show ventricular activation that is not being driven normally by the atria.

  • AVdissociation
    +Show / hide diagram

    P waves march through independently of the wide QRS complexes, demonstrating separate atrial and ventricular rhythms.

  • Ccapture beat
    +Show / hide diagram

    A conducted supraventricular impulse activates the ventricles before the next expected VT beat. The capture QRS resembles the conducted baseline QRS, which need not be narrow if baseline bundle branch block is present.

  • Ffusion beat
    +Show / hide diagram

    A conducted supraventricular impulse and a ventricular impulse activate the ventricles together. The resulting QRS combines features of both activation patterns. It need not be premature or have a particular width. In this schematic, fusion occurs on the VT cycle and VT continues afterward.

These highly specific surface EKG patterns strongly favor an abnormal ventricular activation direction.

02 · Activation physiology

How activation creates the QRS

Most WCT criteria are different ways of asking the same question: where did ventricular activation start, and how did it spread?

A supraventricular impulse usually gains early access to the His-Purkinje system. A ventricular impulse often begins in working myocardium and spreads slowly before recruiting the specialized conduction network. That difference shapes the first part of the QRS.

Follow the first 40 ms

Same width. Different activation.

Compare equally wide 180-ms examples, then switch to a relatively narrow fascicular VT. Follow the red time marker on the EKG and the highlighted regions on the conduction diagram.

SVT with RBBB

Complete QRS (180 ms shown)

Onset: The impulse has reached the His-Purkinje system. Initial septal activation begins.

First 40 ms: The left bundle rapidly activates much of the left ventricle. Right ventricular activation is delayed.

Later activation: Activation spreads toward the delayed right ventricle, contributing to the late R′ in V1.

Early access to an intact bundle can preserve brisk initial forces despite a wide QRS.

SVT with LBBB

Complete QRS (180 ms shown)

Onset: The impulse reaches the right bundle; the usual left-sided activation route is delayed.

First 40 ms: Right ventricular and right-to-left septal activation begin. V1 develops a predominantly negative QRS.

Later activation: Delayed left ventricular activation prolongs the remainder of the QRS.

A different bundle is delayed, so the shape changes even though the overall width is the same.

Myocardial VT

Complete QRS (180 ms shown)

Onset: A ventricular wavefront emerges into working myocardium.

First 40 ms: Early spread through myocardium produces relatively little initial voltage excursion in this example.

Later activation: More myocardium and potentially faster conduction tissue are recruited, producing larger later forces.

The same 180-ms width can conceal a different activation sequence. Slow onset supports VT but is not specific to it.

Fascicular VT

Complete QRS (130 ms shown)

Onset: A ventricular circuit involving the Purkinje network initiates activation.

First 40 ms: Rapid access to conduction tissue creates brisk initial forces despite the ventricular mechanism.

Later activation: Activation completes with a relatively narrow, RBBB-like complex in this example.

Ventricular origin does not require slow initial forces. The 130-ms example is illustrative, not a diagnostic cutoff.

The surface EKG records changing electrical vectors, not conduction velocity directly. Slope and voltage excursion are indirect clues. These diagrams illustrate selected activation patterns, not universal behavior or a validated electrophysiology simulation.

The ventricular cycle is 440 ms and the atrial cycle is 640 ms. Their relationship changes from beat to beat. Some P waves are partly buried in a QRS or T wave.

Four questions behind the algorithms

Each algorithm step below is labeled with the physiologic question it is trying to answer.

01

AV independence

Are the atria and ventricles being driven separately?

AV dissociation, capture beats, and fusion beats provide direct evidence of an independent ventricular rhythm.

Wellens · Brugada · Vereckei · Europace VT score
02

Initial activation velocity

Does the QRS begin quickly or slowly?

RS interval, RWPT-II, initial q or r duration, notching, and vi/vt are surface-signal clues to the time course of activation, not direct conduction-velocity measurements.

Kindwall · Brugada · Vereckei · aVR · Pava
03

Activation direction

Are the initial and overall activation directions plausible?

An initial R in aVR, Northwest Axis, precordial concordance, and absence of any precordial RS complex suggest an abnormal activation vector.

Wellens · Brugada · aVR · Limb Lead Algorithm
04

Bundle branch plausibility

Does the whole QRS look like a real bundle branch block?

Lead-specific morphology asks whether the tracing follows a coherent RBBB or LBBB activation pattern rather than merely looking wide.

Wellens · Kindwall · Brugada · Griffith · Vereckei

Does it look like a bundle branch block?

Choose the broad pattern in V1, then examine the corresponding findings in V1/V2 and V6. The example pairs below show both a typical bundle pattern and a pair that favors VT.

RBBB-like WCT
  • V1: a monophasic R, qR/QR, or biphasic RS pattern favors VT; a taller initial than terminal peak in a double-peaked R is another classic clue.
  • V6: R/S <1, QS, or QR favors VT.
  • For Brugada step 4, look for the relevant morphology findings in both the right precordial lead and V6. One isolated unusual lead is not the paired rule.

    04 · Pitfalls and exceptions

    Know when the rules can bend

    The surface algorithms are useful, but their assumptions can fail in recognizable ways. Compare the baseline EKG and interpret a negative criterion cautiously when one of these settings is present.

    01

    Purkinje-mediated VT

    Fascicular VT

    Because the circuit recruits the specialized conduction system, the initial QRS may be brisk and the total QRS relatively narrow. Posterior fascicular VT often has RBBB morphology with a left superior axis.

    Pitfall: it can resemble SVT with aberrancy and escape slow-onset criteria.
    +Show / hide diagram

    This schematic matches a common posterior fascicular pattern: RBBB-like precordial morphology with a left superior frontal axis. Fascicular VT has important variants.

    02

    Atrial activity

    VT with 1:1 VA conduction

    Some VTs conduct backward through the AV node on every beat. The resulting P waves have a fixed relationship to each QRS even though the rhythm begins in the ventricles.

    Pitfall: 1:1 atrioventricular association does not establish SVT.
    +Show / hide diagram

    Each ventricular complex is followed by retrograde atrial activity at a fixed interval. The association is 1:1, but ventricular activation still comes first.

    03

    Accessory pathway

    Pre-excited tachycardia

    An antegrade accessory pathway activates ventricular myocardium outside the His-Purkinje system. A supraventricular rhythm can therefore begin slowly and produce an unusual wide QRS.

    Pitfall: the activation physiology can imitate myocardial VT.
    +Show / hide diagram

    Pre-excitation starts ventricular activation outside the normal His-Purkinje entry point, so a supraventricular tachycardia can have slow initial forces.

    04

    Baseline matters

    BBB, IVCD, and ventricular pacing

    Pre-existing conduction disease can make an SVT look atypical, while ventricular pacing follows a different activation pathway altogether. A baseline 12 lead EKG can reveal the patient’s usual QRS pattern.

    Pitfall: unfamiliar baseline morphology may satisfy VT criteria.
    +Show / hide diagram

    A baseline tracing can show that an unusual wide QRS is the patient's usual conduction pattern. Similarity informs the differential but is not absolute proof of mechanism.

    05

    Conduction slowing

    Drugs and electrolytes

    Sodium-channel blocking drugs and metabolic disturbances such as hyperkalemia can slow myocardial conduction and widen a supraventricular QRS beyond its usual bundle branch pattern.

    Pitfall: slow initial activation is not unique to VT.
    +Show / hide diagram

    Both examples are schematic. Drug and electrolyte effects can slow supraventricular conduction enough to imitate criteria based on QRS width or initial velocity.

    06

    The conduction system can sustain VT

    Bundle branch reentrant VT

    The bundle branches can form part of a ventricular reentry circuit. The QRS may resemble an ordinary bundle branch block, sometimes even matching the baseline pattern.

    Pitfall: a familiar bundle pattern or brisk initial forces do not independently exclude VT.
    +Show / hide diagram
    01Down one bundle branch→
    02Across ventricular connections→
    03Back up the other bundle

    The His-Purkinje system participates in the ventricular reentry circuit. This is a conceptual loop, not a recording of conduction times. A typical bundle branch block pattern does not exclude this mechanism; EP evidence establishes the circuit.

    07

    Outside this atlas

    Irregular, polymorphic, paced, or artifactual

    These algorithms were developed primarily for regular monomorphic WCT. An irregular or changing QRS, pacing spikes, or suspected artifact should redirect the interpretation.

    Pitfall: forcing an out-of-scope tracing through a binary algorithm.
    +Show / hide diagram
    01Irregular or changing QRS→
    02Pacing or artifact→
    03Use a different pathway

    Confirm rhythm regularity, morphology stability, pacing status, and signal quality before applying an algorithm designed for regular monomorphic WCT.

    Synthetic case 3

    The fast-network exception

    Synthetic scenario: a young adult without known structural heart disease has regular tachycardia. QRS duration is 130 ms, with RBBB-like V1 morphology and superiorly directed inferior-lead forces.

    Does the relatively narrow, sharp QRS exclude VT?

    Reveal reasoning and annotated EKG

    No. This combination should raise the possibility of posterior fascicular VT.

    1. A fascicular circuit recruits specialized conduction tissue, so ventricular activation need not start slowly.
    2. RBBB-like morphology with a left superior axis is a common posterior fascicular pattern.
    3. The full limb-lead set would be needed to establish the frontal axis; a negative lead II alone is not enough.

    Synthetic case 4

    One P wave for every QRS

    Synthetic scenario: each wide complex is followed by a negative P wave in lead II. The relationship appears perfectly regular.

    Does the 1:1 association tell you this is SVT?

    Reveal reasoning and annotated EKG

    No. VT can conduct retrogradely to the atria on every beat.

    1. The depicted VA interval is fixed at 220 ms.
    2. In the constructed mechanism, ventricular activation precedes and drives atrial activation.
    3. A surface 1:1 relationship can occur in more than one mechanism. Additional morphology, onset, or EP evidence is needed.

    Tempting mistake: The absence of visible AV dissociation is not evidence that the ventricles must be driven by the atria.

    How the diagnosis is established: The causal direction is specified in this synthetic example; it is not inferred solely from the apparent position of a P wave in a real tracing.
